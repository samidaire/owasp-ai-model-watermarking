Chapter 1

1Sigil in one page

Sigil hides a cryptographic watermark inside a neural network's weights, then proves who owns the model with a zero-knowledge proof. Anyone can check the proof; nobody learns the watermark.

The problem

A trained model is expensive: data, compute and expertise. Once a copy leaks, or a customer passes it on, the owner needs a way to say "that model is mine" and have a third party (a court, a marketplace, an auditor) check it.

A white-box watermark does part of this: during training, the owner nudges one layer's weights so that a secret key reads out a secret bit pattern (the signature). Later the owner extracts the pattern from a suspect copy. But this has three weaknesses, and the demo shows each one happening:

  • Checking reveals the secret. To verify, the checker normally needs the key and signature, and whoever has them can scrub the mark from the model.
  • Anyone can claim anything. A thief can invent a key that "finds" their own message in the stolen model, or even in a model that was never watermarked (demo C: the thief reads out "MALLORY!" with zero errors).
  • A valid proof isn't enough. A zero-knowledge proof only says "some computation was correct". Without binding it to the owner, the exact model and the moment of the claim, a forged or replayed proof also verifies (demo E).

What Sigil does

01EmbedA secret seed generates the key and a 64-bit signature. Training hides the signature in one layer. Accuracy barely moves.
02CommitBefore release, the owner publishes a Poseidon hash of the seed, signs it with their Ed25519 key and gets a registry timestamp.
03ProveA Circom circuit re-derives the key and signature from the committed seed and checks the suspect's weights. SnarkJS turns that into a Groth16 proof.
04VerifyAnyone checks the proof in milliseconds, off-chain or on an Ethereum-style chain. Zero watermark bits are revealed.

Who it's for

  • Model owners who want to protect a model before releasing it.
  • Verifiers (courts, marketplaces, auditors) who need to check an ownership claim without learning the owner's secrets.
  • Examiners and researchers: the six research demos (A–F) walk through the attacks on naive schemes and how each fix closes them.
Chapter 2

2Key ideas in plain words

The app uses these terms everywhere. Each is explained without maths first; chapter 3 adds the detail.

TermWhat it means here
WeightsThe numbers a neural network learns during training. The demo model is a small convolutional network for MNIST handwritten digits.
Watermarked layerThe layer that carries the mark: conv2. Its weights are averaged into a list of 288 numbers (the "Uchida vector", after the method's inventor).
SignatureThe secret bit pattern hidden in the layer: 64 bits (T = 64) in the demo; 256 is the security setting.
Key XA secret 64 × 288 table of +1/−1 values. Multiplying it by the layer's 288 numbers gives 64 results; each result's sign is one extracted bit.
Secret seedA 248-bit random number that generates both the key and the signature. It never leaves the owner's machine (stored encrypted).
BER (bit error rate)The fraction of the 64 bits that read out wrong. 0.000 means a perfect match; about 0.5 means random (no watermark).
Threshold θThe acceptance rule: at most 8 of 64 bits may be wrong (θ = 0.125). In protocol v2 the circuit enforces it.
CommitmentA hash of the seed (plus the owner's key and the settings) published before release. It reveals nothing about the seed, and the seed can't be swapped for another one later.
PoseidonA hash function designed to be cheap inside zero-knowledge circuits. Sigil's Python version matches circomlib bit-for-bit.
Owner identityAn Ed25519 key pair (elliptic-curve signatures). The owner signs their registration with it, and the seed's key and signature depend on it.
Registry receiptA timestamp signed by a registry: "this registration existed at this time". When two parties claim the same model, the earliest valid registration wins.
Claim and nonceThe verifier opens a claim about a suspect file with a fresh random number (the nonce). The proof must include it, so an old proof can't be replayed.
w_hashA hash of the suspect model's layer values, computed inside the proof and recomputed by the verifier from its own copy of the file. It ties the proof to that exact model.
zk-SNARK, Groth16A short proof that a computation was done correctly on secret inputs, revealing nothing else. Groth16 is the proof system used: 3 elliptic-curve points (879 bytes as JSON).
Circom, SnarkJSCircom describes the computation as a circuit (circuits/ownership.circom); SnarkJS creates and checks the proofs.
Trusted setupGroth16 needs public parameters made once per circuit. Phase 1 uses the public Perpetual Powers of Tau ceremony; phase 2 is one contribution for Sigil's circuit.
On-chain verifierA Solidity smart contract exported by SnarkJS that checks the same proof on an Ethereum-style chain. The demo runs it on a local Hardhat chain.
Protocol v1 / v2v1 is the research baseline (demos A–E). v2 is the full fix (demo F and the SDK). See §3.7.
Replay (simulate) modeThe app shows the saved results and the Run buttons replay recorded logs; nothing executes. Live mode runs the real scripts on your machine.
Chapter 3

3How it works

Four parties, four steps, and eleven checks. The full specification is docs/protocol.md.

3.1 Who is involved

PartyHoldsTrusted for
OwnerThe secret seed and an Ed25519 private keyNothing: the protocol assumes the prover may be malicious (for example, claiming someone else's model)
RegistryAn Ed25519 registry keyIssuing honest timestamps (receipts)
VerifierThe registry's public keys and its own copy of the suspect modelNothing beyond running the checks
ThiefThe stolen weights; may register their own seed and key, fine-tune, prune or overwrite—

3.2 Step 1 · Embed the watermark

  1. The owner creates an Ed25519 key pair (their identity) and a random seed.
  2. From (seed, owner key), a Poseidon-based generator derives the key X (64 × 288 values of ±1) and the signature b (64 bits). Because both depend on the owner's key, a thief can't reuse someone else's seed with their own identity.
  3. Training adds a small extra loss so that, for the conv2 layer's 288-value vector w, the 64 signs of X · w spell out b. In the demo this costs 0.03 percentage points of accuracy (98.69% → 98.66%; 98.57% for the v2 model).

3.3 Step 2 · Commit and register (before release)

  1. The owner computes the commitment C = Poseidon(seed, owner key hash, settings hash). The settings (64 bits, layer conv2, at most 8 errors, fixed-point scale) are inside it, so they can't be loosened later.
  2. The owner signs a registration: public key, settings, commitment, the model file's SHA-256 and the time.
  3. A registry checks the registration and signs a receipt with the time it saw it. The seed stays on the owner's machine, encrypted (scrypt + AES-256-GCM).

3.4 Step 3 · Prove ownership of a suspect model

  1. The verifier opens a claim about a suspect file: it records the file's SHA-256 and a fresh random nonce.
  2. The owner runs the circuit. Secret inputs: the seed and the suspect's layer values (as fixed-point integers). Public inputs: owner key hash, settings hash, commitment, w_hash, nonce.
  3. Inside the circuit: re-derive X and b from the seed; recompute the commitment and check it equals the public one; hash the layer values into w_hash; extract the 64 bits; count mismatches with b; enforce at most 8; bind the nonce.
  4. SnarkJS outputs a Groth16 proof: 879 bytes. The circuit has 116,822 constraints; on the demo laptop the witness takes 1.43 s and the proof 3.73 s.

3.5 Step 4 · Verify

The verifier accepts only if every check passes. Each failure reports its own reason, which is what the app's What gets checked list shows.

Check (as shown in the app)What it rules out
Registration signed by the ownerA registration made up or edited by someone else
Registry receipt from a trusted registryA fake or self-made timestamp
Registered before the claimRegistering a model only after the dispute started
Claim refers to this registrationMixing a proof with another owner's registration
Claim refers to this suspect fileProving something about a different file than the one in dispute
Proof is for the owner's keyUsing someone else's identity
Same watermark configurationProving with looser settings (for example, more allowed errors)
Opens the published commitmentUsing a seed other than the one committed before release
About this exact model (w_hash)Reusing a proof about a different model; the verifier recomputes w_hash from its own copy

3.6 Why a thief can't win

  • They can't open the owner's commitment without the seed, so they can't prove the owner's registration.
  • Registering their own seed afterwards doesn't help: their own key reads 28 of 64 bits wrong on the stolen model (limit 8), so no proof is possible, and their registration would be later anyway.
  • Searching for a lucky seed is hopeless at scale: each try succeeds with probability about 2.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. Demo D tried 20,000 seeds: 0 accepted, best 16 of 64 wrong. At T = 256 the odds become about 2⁻¹²⁰.
  • Overwriting (embedding their own mark on top) can give both sides a valid proof. The registry timestamp settles it: the earliest valid registration wins (demo F6).

3.7 Protocol v1 vs v2

v1 · research baseline (demos A–E)v2 · the full fix (demo F, SDK)
CommitmentSHA-256 of seed + owner namePoseidon of seed + Ed25519 key hash + settings
Key and signatureGaussian key from the seed (numpy); shown on screen for teaching±1 key and signature from a Poseidon generator; never shown
Owner identityA text label (owner:yash)Ed25519 signature on the registration
Zero-knowledge proofEZKL (Halo2/KZG) experiments, not bound to model or claimCircom + Groth16; threshold, model (w_hash) and claim (nonce) bound in-circuit

5Installing and starting

Two ways to run it. Option 1 is enough for viewing and presenting; Option 2 makes the Run buttons execute for real.

5.2 Option 1 · Quick start in replay mode

  1. Unzip owasp-wm-demo.zip anywhere, for example into Downloads.
  2. Windows: open the owasp-ai-model-watermarking folder and double-click Start-Demo-UI-Simulate.cmd.
    macOS / Linux: open a terminal in that folder and run python3 demo/server.py --simulate --open.
  3. A console window opens and your browser opens http://127.0.0.1:8765 on the home page. If the browser doesn't open, type that address yourself.
  4. Keep the console window open while you use the app. Closing it (or pressing Ctrl+C in it) stops the app.
What replay mode means

Every page shows the saved results from real runs. The Run buttons replay the recorded output of those runs in the console, so presenting works on any laptop, with no installs and no risk. A banner at the top of the app says Simulate mode so nobody mistakes it for a live run.

5.3 Option 2 · Everything live

  1. Install Python 3.10+ and Node.js 20.19+.
  2. Open a terminal in the owasp-ai-model-watermarking folder (Windows 11: right-click the folder → Open in Terminal).
  3. Run the setup: 
    # Windows (PowerShell)
.\setup.ps1 --demo
# if PowerShell blocks the script:
powershell -ExecutionPolicy Bypass -File setup.ps1 --demo
# macOS / Linux
./setup.sh --demo
    It creates a private Python environment (.venv), installs a CPU build of PyTorch, the demo packages, the sigil SDK and the circuit tools, then installs the proving keys from dist/, checking each against the published records in circuits/keys/.
  4. Start the app: double-click Start-Demo-UI.cmd (Windows) or run .venv/bin/python demo/server.py --open (macOS / Linux).
  5. Check the sidebar's environment chip: it shows your GPU's name or CPU only. If it shows No ML stack: replay only, the setup didn't finish; run it again or use Option 1.

5.4 Stopping, ports and a backup

  • Stop: close the console window, or press Ctrl+C in it.
  • Port in use: start on another port, for example python demo/server.py --port 8770, and open http://127.0.0.1:8770.
  • Backup during a presentation: keep a second terminal ready with python demo/server.py --simulate --port 8766. If a live run misbehaves, switch the browser to http://127.0.0.1:8766.
  • The server only listens on 127.0.0.1: other computers on the network can't reach it.

Chapter 7

7Step-by-step walkthroughs

W1–W5 work in replay and in live mode; W6 shows an examiner that the runs are real. Times are for presenting.

W1 · The 5-minute presentation

  1. Home (/), 30 s: read the headline, "Prove it's yours. Reveal nothing." Point at the weight grid: the caramel cells carry signature bits. Click Open dashboard.
  2. Overview, 45 s: walk the four tiles: 3.73 s to prove, −0.03 pp accuracy, 0 of 20,000 forgeries, 13 of 14 attacks survived. In the models table, show that the clean and null models read "No match".
  3. Embed watermark, 45 s: on protocol v2, point out that the signature is drawn as hidden squares and only the commitment is public. Switch to v1 to show what a revealed signature looks like, then back.
  4. Verify ownership, 90 s: read the claim, show the proof is tiny (three curve points), then the green stamp. Switch to On-chain: 243,225 gas. Click tampered proof bytes: red stamp, and the failing check is highlighted. Click Back to the honest proof.
  5. Robustness lab, 60 s: the pruning chart: "the mark outlives the model". Point at the one red card (feature permutation) and say it's the known limit.
  6. Close with the research demos if there is time (W5).

W2 · Verify a claim and show a rejection

  1. Open Verify ownership from the sidebar.
  2. Read The claim card: which model, whose key, which commitment, when it was registered.
  3. In the proof box, note the five public signals: owner key hash, settings hash, commitment, w_hash, nonce. Nothing secret is in them.
  4. Check the verdict: Ownership verified, 0 of 64 bits revealed. Scroll to What gets checked: all eleven are ✓.
  5. Under Try a misuse, click each button in turn. For each, read the reason on the stamp card and find the red check in the list (see the table in §6.5).
  6. Toggle Off-chain / On-chain to show the same proof checked by the smart contract.

W3 · Run a demo and cancel a run

  1. Open a demo page, for example Demo F, and click its caramel Run button (or Run all live in the sidebar).
  2. The console opens and streams the output. In live mode demo F takes about 1–2 minutes (86 s on a CPU-only laptop); in replay mode about 2 s.
  3. While it runs, the sidebar shows spinners on the affected demos and the other Run buttons are locked.
  4. To stop, click Cancel run in the console. The status turns to "cancelled" and the previous results stay.
  5. When it finishes, the status shows ✓ and every page reloads the new results.
Don't run these live in front of an audience

Retrain (demo A) creates a new owner seed and key, which makes demos B–E stale until re-run. E3 needs about 32 GB of RAM and 10 GB of disk. Both ask for confirmation first; use replay mode or the saved results for them.

W4 · Show what attacks do

  1. Open Robustness lab.
  2. Pick a card, for example int4 quantisation: 100% of bits recovered, accuracy 98.66%. The thief gains nothing.
  3. Scroll to the Pruning sweep: as pruning grows, accuracy (caramel) collapses long before the bits (dark) drop below the dashed threshold.
  4. Show the red feature permutation card: accuracy unchanged, 56.3% of bits (BER 0.438): removed. Then permutation + owner realign: back to 100%.
  5. Click Run attacks live to re-run demo B (about 10 s live).

W5 · Present the research story A → F

  1. Open Research demos → A in the sidebar. Use → (or a clicker) to advance.
  2. A: the owner commits, then embeds; accuracy is unchanged.
  3. B: the mark survives the usual attacks.
  4. C: but without a commitment, a thief forges ownership of anything ("MALLORY!").
  5. D: committing to the seed stops that: 0 of 20,000.
  6. E: adding a ZK proof naively isn't enough: the forged proof verifies too.
  7. F: protocol v2 binds everything (owner key, settings, commitment, model, claim) and closes every gap shown before. Open Presenter notes on each page for the exact wording and caveats.

W6 · Show the teacher it's really live

Replay mode only shows saved results. In live mode every Run button really trains, attacks and proves on the laptop in front of the examiner, and the numbers on screen change. This is how to make that visible.

The day before

  1. On the presentation laptop, run the setup once (§5.3, Option 2). It needs the internet; the presentation itself doesn't.
  2. Start with Start-Demo-UI.cmd, not the Simulate one. Check three things: there is no "Simulate mode" banner, the sidebar card says live mode, and the environment chip shows the GPU or CPU only.
  3. Do one rehearsal run of demo F, so the first run in front of the teacher isn't the first run ever.

In front of the teacher

  1. Note the "before". On Overview, read out "0 / 20,000" forgeries. On Verify ownership, read out the first digits of pi_a in the proof box and the registration time.
  2. Let the teacher choose. Open Demo D, let the teacher pick the number of thief seeds (20,000 or 100,000) and click Run live. The console streams the real search (about 3 s for 20,000, about 17 s for 100,000). Afterwards the Overview tile reads, for example, "0 / 100,000". Measured on this laptop: 100,000 seeds in 16.8 s, 0 accepted.
  3. Generate a fresh proof. Open Demo F and click Run demo F live (about 80 s on a GPU laptop, 86 s CPU-only). Explain the six parts while the console streams them. When it finishes, the Verify page shows a brand-new proof: different pi_a digits, a new nonce, a new registration time, and new entries in the Overview audit log.
  4. Attack it live. On Robustness lab, click Run attacks live (about 10 s): pruning, quantisation and fine-tuning run again and the chart redraws.
  5. Optional, strongest: on Embed watermark → Protocol v1, click Retrain with a new seed… (about 35 s on a GPU). A new owner seed, a new commitment and new signature bits appear. Demos B–E are then marked ! (stale) until you click Run all live (about 30 s), which recomputes them with the new key. Do this last.
What stays precomputed, and why

E3 needs about 32 GB of RAM, so it's shown from the saved run. The on-chain check runs separately from circuits/onchain: after a live demo F run, the On-chain view says it was made for an earlier proof. That's expected; the off-chain verdict is the live one. Live runs overwrite demo/results/; to get the shipped results back, unzip the package again.

8.2 An end-to-end session

# --- owner, before release ---
sigil keygen
sigil registry-keygen                          # a local registry, for testing
sigil seed --bits 64 --layer conv2
# train with sigil.embed.embed_torch(...) (see 8.3), save model.pt
sigil detect model.pt                          # expect: accepted, 0 bits wrong
sigil register model.pt --registry-key secrets/registry.key
#   -> registration.json and registration.receipt.json

# --- later: a verifier checks a suspect copy ---
sigil claim registration.json --suspect suspect.pt              # verifier
sigil prove suspect.pt --claim claim.json                       # owner -> bundle/
sigil verify bundle --suspect suspect.pt --registration registration.json \
      --receipt registration.receipt.json --trusted-registry <registry public key hex>

8.3 From Python

from sigil import protocol as P
from sigil.identity import KeyPair
from sigil.embed import embed_torch

owner = KeyPair.generate()
cfg   = P.Config(T=64, layer="conv2")          # M=288, max_err=8 by default
seed  = P.new_seed()
pkh   = P.pk_hash(owner.public_bytes)
commitment = P.commitment(seed, pkh, cfg)        # publish this before release

# train a torch.nn.Module with the watermark on cfg.layer
model, detection = embed_torch(model, (x_train, y_train), cfg, seed, pkh, epochs=2)
print(detection.errors, detection.accepted)

8.4 Model formats and safety

  • Formats: PyTorch .pt, Keras .h5 / .keras and .onnx. The same weights give the identical layer vector in every format.
  • Safe loading: PyTorch files load with weights_only=True, Keras with safe_mode=True (Lambda layers refused), ONNX through onnx.checker with size limits. --trust turns this off: only for your own files, never for a suspect's.
  • ONNX caveat: exporters fold BatchNorm into the convolution before it, changing that layer. Verify from the original .pt / .keras file, or export without constant folding.
  • Secrets: keep secrets/ off shared drives and out of chats. The key and signature are derived in memory and never written to disk.

Chapter 11

11Honest limits

What the demo does not claim. These are also in the app's Presenter notes, because examiners ask about them.

  • White-box only. The verifier needs the suspect's weights. A copy made only by querying the model's outputs (model extraction) isn't covered by a white-box mark (OWASP limitation L1).
  • Feature permutation reorders channels at no accuracy cost and removes the mark unless the owner realigns before proving.
  • T = 64 is a demo size. Forging by seed search costs about 2³² tries (about 114 single-core hours for a 50% chance): feasible with GPUs. Timestamps stay essential; T = 256 raises it to about 2¹²⁰.
  • The threshold is indicative. 8 of 64 is not a calibrated false-positive rate; four null models can't give one.
  • Overwriting produces two valid marks. The registry timestamp, not the watermark, decides who was first.
  • Trusted setup. Phase 1 uses the public Perpetual Powers of Tau; its full transcript check was too slow for the demo laptop and is recorded as pending in the setup files. Phase 2 has a single contributor. Groth16 is sound if at least one contributor in each phase discarded their randomness.
  • Research demos C and E. Demo C runs ZKROWNN's relation, not ZKROWNN's code (ZKROWNN assumes a semi-honest prover). E3 is the halfway fix and needs about 32 GB of RAM, so it is precomputed.
  • Demo identities are public. Seeds and keys in demo/ckpt/ are committed for reproducibility: anyone with the repository can produce the demo owner's proofs.

Frequently asked

Does it need the internet, or send data anywhere?

No. The app and the server run on your machine and listen only on 127.0.0.1. Only the optional setup downloads packages.

Is replay mode faking the results?

No. The pages show the saved results of real runs, and the console replays their recorded output. The banner says it is replay mode. Note that the recorded console logs and the saved result files come from runs made at slightly different times, so a few printed values can differ by a bit or two (for example, demo F6's owner shows 0 of 64 wrong in the replayed log and 1 of 64 in the saved results). Live mode always shows the current run.

Can I watermark my own model?

Yes, with the SDK (chapter 8): PyTorch training via embed_torch, Keras via the Keras adapter, and detection and verification from .pt, .h5/.keras or .onnx files.

What exactly does the verifier learn?

Five public values: hashes of the owner key and settings, the commitment, the hash of the suspect model's layer, and the nonce the verifier chose itself. Not the seed, the key or a single signature bit.

Why is it called Sigil?

A sigil is a seal: a mark that shows something belongs to someone. Here the seal is hidden in the weights and proven without being shown.
